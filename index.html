Estimate a Livestock Indemnity Program payment

USDA's Livestock Indemnity Program (LIP) pays for cattle that die from extreme weather, certain diseases or an attack by a federally protected predator, beyond the normal death loss for the herd. Enter what you lost to see what LIP may pay. Your county FSA office makes the actual decision.

What to do after a loss

LIP is paid on records. What you write down and keep in the first days after a loss decides what FSA can pay.

1. Right away

  • Count the dead animals by group (cows, bulls, calves by weight) and write down the date and what happened.
  • Take dated photos of the animals and of the damage: flooded pasture, downed fences, storm damage, burned ground.
  • Keep receipts for disposal or rendering. A rendering truck receipt with head and weights is good evidence.
  • For a disease loss, get a veterinarian's report naming the disease and the cause of death.
  • Note which dead cows were bred. FSA asks for breeding or pregnancy check records only if it needs them, so keep them.

2. Show the event happened

  • Weather records from the National Weather Service or NOAA for your area and dates, news reports, or your own weather station's data if it was posted publicly at the time.
  • Extreme heat: FSA's guideline looks for a temperature-humidity index above 84 for two days in a row, without nights cooling below 75, and heat that is unusual for that time of year. Your state FSA committee sets the exact test.
  • Extreme cold: FSA's guideline uses wind chill, for example -20°F for calves under 800 lb and -40°F for cows and bulls, for two days or more. Here too your state committee sets the thresholds.
  • Drought is not a LIP loss (except anthrax that comes with drought). Grazing losses to drought are covered by a different program, LFP: see the Beef Cattle Drought Tracker.

3. Have your herd records ready

  • How many head of each group you had when the event began: calving records, inventories, a balance sheet, purchase and sale receipts.
  • Any cattle that died earlier in the year from normal causes. They count against the normal death loss first.
  • Injured cattle you sold for less: sell within 30 days after the event ended, to an independent buyer (sale barn, packer, renderer), and keep the receipt showing the price, kind, type and weight.
  • Cattle you sold this year at a good price: a receipt can let FSA use your price instead of its national value, up to 145% of it.

4. File with your county FSA office

  • File at the FSA office for the county where the cattle died. FSA wants every head of eligible livestock you had in that county that year accounted for.
  • Form CCC-852, the notice of loss and application for payment, with your records.
  • Income limit: no LIP payment if your average adjusted gross income is over $900,000, unless at least 75% of it comes from farming, ranching or forestry (form CCC-943). There is no cap on the payment itself.
  • Your county office may also need its usual eligibility forms on file; ask what they have for you.

What LIP covers

  • Weather: tornadoes, hurricanes and tropical storms, floods, straight-line winds, lightning, hail, blizzards, winter storms of three days or more, wildfires, extreme heat, extreme cold and earthquakes, when the weather was unusual for that time of year.
  • Diseases made worse by weather: anthrax, blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) and larkspur poisoning; and insect-spread diseases with no vaccine or management practice to prevent them, such as bluetongue, EHD, Cache Valley virus and Theileria.
  • Attacks by animals reintroduced or protected by federal law, including wolves and protected birds. Starting in 2026 these pay 100% of market value. Ask your county office whether an attack on your calves qualifies before you count on it.
  • Cattle that are bred: starting in 2024, LIP also pays for the unborn calf of a bred cow it pays on.

Sources: FSA's 2026 LIP fact sheet; 7 CFR 1416 subpart D as amended July 9, 2026; FSA Handbook 1-LIP.

About this estimate

How the page works out a payment, where every number comes from, and what it can't know.

How it's figured

  1. Normal death loss: for each group, head on hand when the event began times the normal mortality rate, rounded down. Cattle that died earlier in the year from normal causes use up that allowance first; what's left is taken off the event's losses.
  2. Payment rate: FSA's market value for the group times 75% (weather or disease) or 100% (protected predator). Your own documented sale price replaces FSA's value when it is higher, up to 145% of it.
  3. Death payment: head paid on times the payment rate.
  4. Unborn calves: for each bred cow paid on, 85% of the value of the lightest calf group, times 75% or 100%. This page counts the bred cows among the cows paid on first; FSA may count them differently.
  5. Injured and sold: head sold times the payment rate, less what the sale brought, never below zero.
  6. Each line times your share of the cattle.

7 CFR 1416.306 as amended July 9, 2026 (Federal Register doc. 2026-13878); FSA Handbook 1-LIP, paragraphs 4 and 25; FSA Notice LIP-14. The page's arithmetic is tested against FSA's own worked examples.

FSA's market values

FSA sets these each year from the year before's market data and announces them between February and April. It also figures end-of-year values; when those come out higher, FSA pays the difference on approved claims.

Normal mortality

FSA Handbook 1-LIP, amendment 9 (posted Sept. 22, 2025), Exhibit 4, National Normal Mortality Rate Table; paragraph 4 A for how state committees set their own.

What this page can't know

  • Your state's own normal mortality rates and heat and cold thresholds, set by your state FSA committee.
  • Whether FSA accepts the event, the cause and your records, and how many head it accepts.
  • FSA's end-of-year market values, any reduction for other compensation, and your eligibility for payment (income limit and FSA's usual forms).
